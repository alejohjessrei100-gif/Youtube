# #16: The Girl Who Was Mailed to Her Grandma

Status: draft, waiting for approval. Every fact is checked (sources below).

## Script (read word for word)

In 1914, a little girl got mailed to her grandma, with the stamps stuck on her coat.

Her name is May. She's five, she lives in Grangeville, Idaho, and Grandma lives seventy miles away.

The train ticket costs real money. And May's parents would really rather not spend it.

But the Post Office just launched something new. Parcel post. Any package up to fifty pounds.

So her dad does the math. May weighs forty-eight and a half.

He carries her up to the counter, and the clerk just stares.

"Sir, that's a child."

"Show me the rule that says I can't."

And he can't. You see, parcel post was so new, nobody had written down that people aren't packages.

So they weigh her, stick fifty-three cents of stamps on her coat, and load her into the mail car with the sacks of letters.

She rides seventy miles with the mail clerk, and gets delivered straight to Grandma's front door.

The Post Office was not amused. Soon after, it made a brand-new rule: no more mailing people.

Which is a shame, because it was the only package in history that said thank you when it arrived.

## Fact check
| claim | status |
|---|---|
| February 19, 1914, Grangeville to Lewiston, Idaho, about 70–75 miles | ✓ Smithsonian National Postal Museum, Idaho newspapers |
| May Pierstorff, age 5 (some sources say 4–6) | ✓ The script says "five", the most common figure |
| 53 cents of parcel post stamps on her coat | ✓ |
| Done to avoid the train fare | ✓ |
| Parcel post began January 1, 1913; the 50 lb limit started January 1, 1914 | ✓ |
| She weighed 48½ lb, just under the limit | ✓ |
| Rode in the train's mail car with the mail clerk, delivered to her grandmother | ✓ |
| No rule existed against mailing people; the Post Office then banned it | ✓ The exact ban date is disputed (1913 vs 1920 reports), so the script says "soon after" |
| "Classified as a baby chick" | ✗ Left out. The USPS historian says chicks weren't mailable until 1918 |
