He opens every door of the cabinet. Gears. Cogs. A red cushion. You can see straight through it.
Nobody's inside.
Then the machine beats you at chess.
It's 1770. A life-sized figure in a turban sits behind the cabinet, and no one can work out how it plays.
For eighty-four years it tours Europe and America. It beats most challengers, even Napoleon.
Writers try to explain it. Edgar Allan Poe publishes a famous essay. Many of his guesses are wrong.
The trick is in the doors. Inside, a chess master sits on a sliding seat. As each door opens, he slides aside, and fake machinery slides into his place.
By candlelight, he moves the pieces with levers and magnets.
In 1854, a fire destroys the machine.
Three years later, the last owner's son finally explains it.
The machine is gone, he writes. There's no longer any reason to hide the secret.
