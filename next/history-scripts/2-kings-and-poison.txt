A king sits down to dinner, and before he eats, someone else tastes his food. That was the first defense against poison, and it was a real job. In Rome the taster was called a praegustator. Emperor Claudius had one, a man named Halotus. Claudius was allegedly poisoned anyway. So how did rulers actually try to survive? King Mithridates of Pontus, whose own father was poisoned, swallowed small doses on purpose, hoping his body would learn to resist. Others trusted a stone from an animal's stomach, the bezoar, said to cure any poison. A French royal surgeon, Ambroise Paré, decided to test it. A cook sentenced to death chose poison over the noose, as long as he got the stone afterwards. He got the stone. He died seven hours later.
